Inside Punjab

Punjab lies in the Punjab plains, and the land runs plain. That single fact decides a lot: what can be grown, how towns are shaped, and what a visitor should pack. Chandigarh is the administrative centre, but the character of the state is set by the district towns rather than the capital — and that is where the markets, the coaching centres and most state-level hiring actually sit.

The languages in daily use are Punjabi · Hindi. That combination matters if you are moving here for a course or a job: the language of the classroom and the language of the workplace are often not the same, and state-level hiring tends to happen in the regional language first, with English alongside.

What Punjab grows and makes

What leaves Punjab for the rest of the country falls into two groups. The fields are built around wheat, rice, potato, kinnow. Alongside them sit the trades and industries that carry the rest of the economy: textiles, sports goods, engineering, dairy.

Read together, those two lists are the honest answer to what kind of work exists here — and they are why the city guides below break employment down sector by sector instead of quoting one statewide number that would mean nothing in any particular town.

When to go

October – March is the working window for a visit. The plain winter — mustard fields, dhaba season and melas across the towns. Day-to-day weather follows the terrain, so plain is the honest way to judge what you are walking into: put outdoor plans in the middle of the day and the markets, food and festivals in the evening.

What people eat in Punjab

Four plates cover most of what a visitor is actually served in Punjab.

morning
Chhole–bhature counters

The Punjab breakfast — chhole with fried bhature, onion salad and lassi.

thali
Punjabi dhabas

The highway institution — tandoori roti, dal makhani, paneer bhurji and white butter.

street
Kulcha–chai stalls

The town morning — amritsari kulcha with chole and kadak chai at the tandoor.

sweets
Pinnis and milk cake

The halwai staples — atta pinni, milk cake and gud treats in winter.

Festival calendar

The year in Punjab runs to a rhythm worth planning around. These are the dates that decide when shops close, when trains and hotels fill, and when a visit is worth making at all.

January
Lohri

The bonfire festival — rewri, gajak, popcorn tossed in the fire and bhangra around it.

January–February
Maghi & Basant

The winter-end festivals — maghi fairs, kite skies and yellow sarson walks.

March
Hola Mohalla (belt)

The Nihang martial festival in the Malwa belt — gatka displays and nagar kirtans.

October–November
Bandi Chhor Diwas

The Sikh festival of lights — gilded gurdwaras, nagar kirtan and lamp nights.
